A Field Guide

The Tools

On natural-language processing, large language models, and the state of the art as of this morning.

May 2026  ·  Updated weekly because everything changes weekly

The book in the next room was edited by software that, ten years ago, did not exist. This page exists because if you've read this far, you might want to know what that software is, who makes it, what it costs, and what it draws from the wall.

The terms, briefly

If you've been around the field, skip to the table. If you haven't, the vocabulary tends to put up a fence around the conversation. Here is the fence, with a stile.

  • NLPNatural Language Processing. The umbrella discipline — getting computers to handle human language. It predates LLMs by sixty years and includes spell-checkers, translation systems, search engines, and everything else that touches text.
  • LLMLarge Language Model. A neural network trained to predict the next chunk of text, given some prior text. The "large" refers to the parameter count — billions to trillions of numerical weights — and to the training data, typically a substantial fraction of the public internet plus licensed books.
  • TokenThe unit a model reads and writes. Roughly three-quarters of a word in English. "Hello" is one token; "antidisestablishmentarian" is several. All pricing is per million tokens.
  • Context windowHow much text the model can hold in its head at once — the prompt plus its answer. A 200K-token window is roughly 150,000 English words, or three short novels.
  • GPTGenerative Pretrained Transformer. The architecture most modern LLMs share. OpenAI uses the term in product names; everyone else trains the same underlying thing.
  • Open weightsThe model's trained parameters are downloadable. You can run it yourself on your own hardware, fine-tune it, inspect it. Distinct from open-source: the training code and data may not be public.
  • InferenceRunning a trained model to get an answer (as opposed to training it in the first place). Inference is what costs you money per token and electricity per query.
  • Reasoning modelA model that generates a chain of intermediate "thinking" tokens before answering. Slower, costlier, more accurate on hard problems. Uses 10–70× the energy of a plain query.

What changed, quickly

Three years ago, GPT-4 was the only model worth the name and cost roughly thirty dollars per million tokens of output. That number is now under one dollar at the same quality, and the public list of frontier labs has gone from one to roughly nine — split across the United States, China, and Europe — with a self-hostable open-weights tier closing in fast on the closed frontier.

Four observations from May 2026:

The price gap between American and Chinese frontier models is now 5 to 25× at equivalent benchmark scores. DeepSeek V4-Pro, released in late April under an MIT license, scores 80.6% on SWE-bench Verified — within striking distance of Claude Opus and GPT-5.5 — at one-twentieth the per-token price.

The gap between proprietary and open-weights models, on most benchmarks, has narrowed to noise. Qwen 3.6, DeepSeek V4, GLM-5, and Kimi K2.6 are all genuinely frontier-class on the work they were trained for. A laptop with a recent GPU can now run a model that, eighteen months ago, would have been the strongest in the world.

The energy question, which dominated headlines in 2024, has settled. A typical text query draws roughly a quarter to a third of a watt-hour — about a second of microwave time. Reasoning queries draw more; coding-agent sessions a great deal more. But the median chatbot conversation is, in energy terms, a rounding error against any hour spent watching streaming video. The water question is similar.

Specialization has replaced supremacy. No single model wins every category. Claude leads at writing and code review; GPT-5.5 at structured reasoning and computer-use agents; Gemini 3.1 Pro at multimodal and abstract reasoning; DeepSeek and Qwen at price-per-quality; Mistral at output-heavy work and EU compliance.

How to choose

For the kind of work that produced this site — cleaning a public-domain text and writing imitation-period prose — any frontier model from the top of the table will do. The differences between them at this task are smaller than the differences in your prompts.

For most personal use, a mid-tier model (Claude Sonnet, GPT-5.4, Gemini 3 Flash, Mistral Medium, Qwen 3.6-72B) is enough and costs under a fifth as much as the top tier. The frontier models matter when you're doing genuinely hard reasoning, very long context work, or anything where the tail of quality matters more than the average.

For privacy-sensitive work — patient records, legal discovery, anything covered by GDPR — the European models or self-hosted open-weights are the only honest answer. Mistral's EU data residency and Apache-licensed weights are designed for exactly this use.

For high volume on a budget, the open-weights tier — DeepSeek V4, Qwen 3.6, Llama 4 — is now genuinely competitive on quality and unbeatable on cost if you have GPU capacity to spare. The breakeven against API pricing is in the tens of millions of tokens per day.
