On Looms, Letters, and Language Models

Three transitions of human labor, and what survives them.

The history of human work is, in many ways, the history of what we have agreed to stop doing by hand. Each generation inherits from its forebears a set of laborious crafts and, by inventing some new device or method, gradually withdraws from the labor and reorients itself toward whatever the device cannot yet do. We see this pattern again and again as we look back across centuries, and the pattern repeats at scales that can feel almost geological, where one craft slowly silts over with disuse and another emerges in its place. To understand the present moment in the writing of software, where large language models have begun to compose thousands of lines of code at a stroke, it is worth standing back and looking at two earlier transitions of this kind: the long evolution of printed text, and the mechanization of cloth.

The Page

Before Gutenberg, a book was a year of a person's life. Scribes copied manuscripts letter by letter in cold scriptoria, and a single Bible might take a year or more to produce, error and all. The printing press, set with hand-cast type one character at a time, did not abolish that labor so much as relocate it: a compositor still picked up each piece of lead, set it in a stick, and locked the form by hand. To produce a single page of a daily newspaper in 1880 still required a small army of typesetters working through the night.

Then in 1886, Ottmar Mergenthaler's Linotype machine cast entire lines of type from a keyboard, and the labor of the compositor was condensed into a few keystrokes per line. A century later, when Donald Knuth grew frustrated with the typographic decay of his own books, he wrote TeX, and a single author at a desk could produce typographic output that would have required a guild of skilled artisans only decades earlier.

At each step, something was lost and something else was gained. The scribe's marginal flourish, the compositor's subtle kerning, the linotype operator's intuitive feel for the slug, all of these crafts faded. But the question that we ask of writing did not change. We still ask whether the words are true, whether the argument holds, whether the book is worth reading. As the manual labor receded, the value of judgment, of editorial taste, of clarity of thought, did not recede with it. It rose.

The Cloth

The textile industry tells the same story, in cloth instead of ink. For most of human history, the production of fabric was so laborious that an ordinary person owned only a handful of garments in a lifetime. A single yard of cloth required hours of carding, spinning, and weaving on a hand loom, and a single boll of cotton had to be picked clean of its sticky seeds by patient fingers. Eli Whitney's cotton gin in 1793, and the power loom that followed it across the mills of New England and Lancashire, collapsed weeks of labor into hours.

The hand spinner and the cottage weaver could not compete. A trade that had defined the lives of millions of women across millennia, the spinning of thread, was made obsolete in two generations.

What rose in its place was design. The mills could weave a hundred bolts of cloth before lunch, but they could not decide what should be woven. The pattern, the dye, the cut, the fit, the brand, the meaning of the garment in the culture that wore it, all of these became the new battleground. The Industrial Revolution did not abolish the textile worker so much as it shifted the locus of value from the hand at the loom to the eye at the drawing board. The hands that had once spun thread were freed, in time, to do other things, eventually including the design of the looms themselves.

The Code

We are now living through a transition of similar character in the writing of software. For most of the short history of computing, code has been written one line at a time, by a person at a keyboard, in much the same way a compositor set type at a press. The art of the programmer was an art of patience, of holding many small details in the mind at once, of arranging tokens into a working machine through long hours of thought and revision. In the last few years, large language models have begun to compose those tokens in great rolling waves, generating thousands of lines of code from a paragraph of intent, and doing so with a fluency that would have seemed fantastical a decade ago. The programmer who once wrote a function now describes one.

It is tempting, in such moments, to grieve the lost craft. The careful naming of variables, the elegant minimization of a loop, the quiet satisfaction of a cleanly closed bracket, these are real pleasures, and they are the pleasures of a guild whose work is being absorbed by a machine. But the grief, while honest, is not the whole story. The same was true of the scribes who watched the press arrive, and the spinners who watched the mill. What survives such transitions is rarely the labor itself. What survives is the judgment that directs the labor.

The Pattern

A pattern emerges if we hold these three transitions together. In each, a craft of execution is gradually subsumed by a machine, and a craft of intention rises in its place. The compositor gives way to the editor. The hand spinner gives way to the designer. The line-by-line programmer gives way to, well, that is the question, and it is the question of our generation. The answer, as far as we can see it from here, is that the programmer becomes something closer to an architect, an investigator, a person who must understand at a deep level what is actually being asked of the machine and whether the answer the machine has given is the right one.

This is where first principles return. When the labor of execution is cheap, the cost of a wrong premise becomes catastrophic. A scribe who copies a flawed manuscript wastes a year. A compositor who sets a flawed manuscript wastes a week. A linotype operator wastes a day. A model that writes ten thousand lines of code from a flawed specification wastes only minutes, but the flaw in the specification can now propagate through millions of lines before anyone notices. The premium, in such a world, falls on the person who can ask the right question, who can see clearly what the problem actually is, who can think from the ground up about what nature, or the user, or the system, actually requires.

What Becomes Valuable

We have seen this before, in fields that long ago automated their grunt work. The mathematician does not gain stature by computing arithmetic faster than a calculator; the mathematician gains stature by seeing the structure that no calculator can see. The photographer did not become irrelevant when the camera became a phone; the photographer's eye, and the photographer's choice of subject, became more valuable, not less. The architect did not vanish when CAD made drafting easy; the architect's vision of a building, the way it would meet the light at four in the afternoon, became the thing that distinguished one building from another. In each case, what survived was the part of the craft that touches the world directly, that is grounded in observation of how things actually are.

The lesson for our moment is that creativity, in the deep sense, is not the production of novel tokens. It is the act of looking at a problem and seeing it as it is, beneath the conventions and the inherited assumptions, and asking what response the world itself seems to call for. A model can produce ten variations on a known pattern. It cannot, at least not yet, walk into a forest and notice that the trees are dying in a particular way that suggests a particular cause. It cannot sit with a user for an hour and notice that what the user says they want is not what they actually need. It cannot stand in front of a physical machine and feel, in the weight and the warmth of it, that something is about to fail.

These are first-principles capacities, in the most literal sense. They are the capacities that emerge from direct contact with the grain of the world. As the labor of mid-level execution recedes, these capacities, which used to be called genius and were treated as rare, are becoming the ordinary table stakes of useful work. The compositor's hands are gone, and so are the spinner's. The architect's eye and the investigator's mind remain.

The Longer View

It is worth, finally, holding all of this in a longer view. The cotton gin is two and a half centuries old. The Linotype is approaching a century and a half. TeX is older than most of the people who use it. Each was greeted in its day with the same mixture of wonder and dread that now greets the language model. Each reshaped the work of millions, and each, in the end, was absorbed into the ordinary furniture of life, leaving behind a world in which more people could read more books, wear more clothes, and write more software than any of their ancestors would have believed possible. There is no reason to think the present transition will end differently.

The labor will move. The judgment will remain. And what we do with the time that the machines have given back to us, what new things we choose to look at clearly and think about from the ground up, will be the measure of what kind of generation we turn out to have been.
