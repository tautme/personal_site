On Leisure Inflation

The disappearing hour, and what it costs the republic.

Self-government requires self-governing time: hours that belong to the citizen, not the employer, not the algorithm.

In 1955, a Hewlett-Packard engineer in Palo Alto closed his lab notebook at five, drove eight minutes home through orchards that had not yet become office parks, and ate dinner with his family. The workweek was forty hours and it ended. There was no email because there was no internet. There were no “work socials” because work was not yet a lifestyle. He read the paper. He talked to his neighbors. On weekends he did nothing of professional consequence, and nobody expected him to.

In 2025, a Google engineer in Mountain View — geographically next door, historically a different planet — leaves the office at six-thirty, sits in seventy minutes of traffic on the 101, and arrives home to a Slack notification that requires “a quick look.” Dinner is eaten with a phone face-down on the table, vibrating. The forty-hour week is a polite fiction; the real boundary is wherever exhaustion finally wins. Work-social events — offsites, team dinners, hackathons, a colleague's “casual” birthday drinks — colonize the evenings that used to be private. The job is not a place. It is an atmosphere.

The Disappearing Hour

Call it leisure inflation: the same nominal hours of “free time” purchase steadily less actual freedom. An hour after work in 1955 was an hour. An hour after work in 2025 is contested territory — interrupted by notifications, mortgaged to fitness optimization, monitored by an inner voice asking whether you should be answering that email instead. The currency has been debased.

Tocqueville's Warning

The political consequence is what should worry us. Tocqueville, traveling through 1830s America, argued that democracy survived not because of its institutions on paper but because of the dense civic life beneath them — the town meetings, the volunteer associations, the newspapers read in common, the long evenings in which ordinary citizens actually thought about the republic they were running. Self-government, he saw, requires self-governing time: hours that belong to the citizen, not the employer, not the algorithm.

That time is now scarce. The average American voter in 2025 enters the booth — or, more often, the mail-in envelope — having not read a ballot measure carefully, having absorbed campaign claims through fifteen-second video clips between work tasks, having outsourced political reasoning to whichever pundit shares their tribe. This is not stupidity. It is depletion. A citizenry whose attention has been strip-mined by work and platforms cannot perform the cognitive labor democracy quietly assumes of it. Tocqueville feared the tyranny of the majority. The subtler danger now is the abdication of the majority — not voting against its interests so much as no longer having the bandwidth to locate them.

What the Republic Requires

The 1955 HP engineer was not more virtuous than the 2025 Google engineer. He was simply richer in the one resource democracy cannot synthesize: unspoken-for time. Returning that resource — through shorter weeks, real boundaries, defended weekends, and a refusal to confuse busyness with worth — is not a lifestyle question. It is a precondition for the kind of citizenship the founders, and Tocqueville after them, took for granted.
